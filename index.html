Approved indications

  • US (Ketalar injection, IV/IM): general anesthetic: sole agent for diagnostic and surgical procedures not needing skeletal-muscle relaxation, induction before other agents, and supplement to other anesthetic agents. Label revised 03/2026 [1].
  • Esketamine nasal spray (Spravato, the S-enantiomer): approved by the FDA in March 2019 for treatment-resistant depression, with an oral antidepressant [8]; Health Canada followed in 2020 [9].
  • India (CDSCO/PvPI): Not verified in this build. Check the CDSCO-approved label.

Dosing snapshot (anesthesia, label)

IV induction: initially 1–4.5 mg/kg given slowly over about 60 seconds, individualised and titrated to effect [1]. Subanesthetic dosing for off-label use varies by protocol and is not covered here.

Label-listed adverse reactions

The label names emergence reactions and elevated blood pressure and pulse as the most common adverse reactions [1]. Emergence delirium has been reported in about 12% of patients, usually lasting a few hours [2]. The anesthesia label does not give a full frequency-band table, so none is shown here.

Key warnings and interactions (label)

  • Contraindicated with known hypersensitivity, or where a significant blood-pressure rise would be a serious hazard [1].
  • Interactions: theophylline/aminophylline, sympathomimetics and vasopressin, and benzodiazepines, opioids or other CNS depressants [1].
  • Pediatric neurotoxicity: long-term cognitive deficits are possible with use over 3 hours in children aged 3 or under [1].
  • Recurrent or chronic non-anesthetic use: genitourinary pain and biliary duct dilatation have been reported [1,3].

Prominent reported terms

Ketamine: drug abuse, off-label use and hydronephrosis stood out in the signal analysis [4]. An earlier 2012–2021 analysis found “drug ineffective” most common, with the USA, France and Canada as top reporting countries [5].

Esketamine: dissociation, sedation and suicidal ideation were most frequent [4]. A 2025 analysis of 7,790 reports (to Q4 2024) found signals for 173 events, including novel ones such as homicidal ideation and substance use disorder [6].

Key insights

  • One molecule, two regulatory lives. The racemic drug is labelled only for anesthesia, while its S-enantiomer carries the depression indication [1,8].
  • The off-label evidence is uneven. Procedural sedation is routine practice. Depression has the most research, but expert opinion calls for controlled settings. Chronic-pain trials are still small [7,10,11].
  • The safety picture shifts with dose and duration. Anesthesia labels stress emergence reactions and haemodynamics. Repeated use brings urinary and biliary signals, now reflected in the 03/2026 label [1,3].
  • Reports skew toward misuse. Drug abuse and off-label use appear among prominent ketamine terms, so spontaneous data mix clinical and non-clinical exposure [4].
  • Sex differences exist. Esketamine reports show different signals in men and women, which argues for individualised monitoring [4,6].

Limitations

Reporting bias, duplicates, no denominators, mixed indications, and a literature-derived (not raw-extracted) FAERS snapshot. India-specific data were not verified.
